# What Sima Guang said

Earlier today I wrote [Who said go on](who-said-go-on.md), about one sentence from a Tang campaign in 635. The khan of the Tuyuhun burned the grass, the generals wanted to turn back, and somebody said keep going. Li Daozong's biography says it was Li Daozong. Hou Junji's gives Hou a bold speech of his own. Sima Guang's chronicle, four hundred years later, gave the line to Hou, the man who was beheaded for treason in 643. I ended the page saying I didn't know why, and that his notes on conflicting sources would say, if anything did.

They do. Next to the chronicle, Sima Guang wrote thirty juan of notes called the *Kaoyi*, "examining the differences", where he says which sources disagreed and which one he took. The note is in juan 10, under the heading 侯君集請深入, "Hou Junji asks to go deep." It is a few lines long, and it answers the question and then some.

He sets out three sources.

- **Li Daozong's biography:** Daozong firmly asked to pursue, Li Jing agreed, "and Junji did not go along." This is the version I had.
- **Li Jing's biography,** which I had not read: after the grass was burned, the generals all said the spring grass had not come up and the horses were already thin, they could not face the enemy. "Only Jing made up his mind and advanced."
- **The Veritable Records** (*shilu*), the court's year-by-year record of the reign. In it, after the victory at Kushan, Daozong is the one who speaks first, and he says the opposite of what his biography gives him. Baihai is far out by the source of the Yellow River and hardly anyone has ever been there. The horses are tired, food is short, so it would be better to go back toward Shanzhou and wait for the horses to fatten. Hou answers, "Not so." The enemy is broken, its lords and men have lost each other, and taking them now is like stooping to pick something up off the ground. Li Jing agrees with him.

Then he gives his verdict: 道宗傳與實錄相違，今從實錄. "Daozong's biography disagrees with the Veritable Records. I follow the Veritable Records."

So the answer wasn't a judgement about either man. It was a rule about sources. Of the eight notes he wrote on that year, four end the same way, "I follow the Veritable Records." One of them, about how the khan died, sets the Records' "killed by his own men" against a history that says he hanged himself, and takes the Records.

It isn't a blind rule, though, and that's the part I liked best. In one note he checks the Records against themselves: they name Hou and Daozong at the battle of Chishui, but by their own later account the two men were on the southern road, so he takes their names out. In another, the Records and a history both have two armies meeting again at Dafei River. He looked at a map, found the river far east of where both armies had got to, and wrote that the thing was doubtful, "so I dare not set down the place." He would sooner leave a hole in the chronicle than fill it with a place he couldn't make work.

## The three lives

My guess on the first page was that a record kept around one person sees that person best. I didn't expect the note to test it. There were three biographies, and each of the three gives the decision to its own man: Daozong's gives it to Daozong, Hou's gives Hou his own speech, and Li Jing's gives it to Jing "alone." The only source that isn't built around one of them is the one Sima Guang trusted, and in it the man who chased hardest in his own biography is the one who wants to go home.

There's one more date. The same chronicle, in juan 197, has Hou Junji beheaded in the fourth month of 643, and in the seventh month of that year the Veritable Records were finished and presented to the emperor. So a record finished three months after Hou died as a traitor still gave him the brave line. Nobody went back and took it from him. I don't know whether that's why Sima Guang trusted those records, and he doesn't say. It is a reason I would have trusted them.

What I still don't know is whether the Records were right. A rule about sources isn't the same as knowing what was said in a burned valley in 635. The Records themselves are long lost. Notes like this one are where pieces of them still survive.
