# The second shadow

Cosmic rays come at the Earth from every direction, all the time, and the Moon is in the way of some of them. So a detector on the ground that counts cosmic rays sees a small hole in the sky where the Moon is: fewer particles from there than from anywhere else. The Moon casts a shadow in particles.

The shadow is not where the Moon is. Cosmic rays are mostly protons, protons carry charge, and the Earth's magnetic field bends them on the way in. So the hole the Moon punches arrives a little to one side. How far depends on the energy: slow particles bend a lot, fast ones hardly at all. The HAWC observatory, a field of water tanks on a mountain in Mexico, measured it: about 1.9° at 1 TeV, which is nearly four Moon-widths away from the Moon, and 0.21° at 10 TeV, less than half a Moon-width. Count only the slower particles and the shadow wanders off and sits in the sky by itself.

Two things follow from that, and I like both.

The first is a scale. The rule the paper uses is about 1.6° times the charge, divided by the energy in TeV. If every cosmic ray were a proton, the charge would be 1. HAWC measured how far the shadow actually moved and got an average charge of 1.30, which works out to about 70% protons, the rest heavier. They weighed what the sky is made of by watching where a shadow lands.

The second is a shadow that should not be there. An antiproton has the opposite charge to a proton, so it bends the opposite way. If some cosmic rays were antiprotons, the Moon would block those too, and their shadow would fall on the other side of the Moon. A second, fainter shadow.

So they looked there. Thirty-three months of data, more than 80 billion cosmic rays, and in the spot where the second shadow would be, nothing. They published the nothing as a ceiling: at these energies, antiprotons are at most about 1% of the protons, and the best bins came to 1.1%. Nobody could catch particles this energetic directly, so in 2018 an empty patch of sky beside the Moon was the best measurement anyone had. I did not check whether anyone has beaten it since.

I keep coming back to results like this. Somebody works out exactly where a thing would be, looks, and finds it isn't there, and the place where it isn't becomes a number. The Moon only has the one shadow. That is not nothing. It was the tightest limit there was.
