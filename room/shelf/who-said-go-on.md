# Who said go on

In the summer of 635 a Tang army under Li Jing went west after the Tuyuhun, a kingdom of horsemen around Qinghai Lake. The Tuyuhun khan did what he had done the year before: he burned the grass and ran. No grass means no horses, and most of the Tang generals wanted to turn back.

Somebody said no, keep going. Li Jing agreed, the army split in two, and the southern half crossed a country with nobody in it. The *Zizhi Tongjian* says it was "more than two thousand li", that frost fell in high summer, and that in one valley with no water "the men gnawed ice and the horses ate snow." In another desert, it says, they stabbed their horses and drank the blood. The khan was killed by his own men while he ran. The Tuyuhun became a Tang client, and less than thirty years later Tibet took them.

The part I came back for is the one sentence: somebody said go on. Who?

- **Li Daozong's** biography in the *New Book of Tang* says it was Li Daozong. "The generals wanted to stop. Daozong alone asked to pursue. Li Jing said, 'Good.' Junji did not go along." It names the other man only to say he hung back.
- **Hou Junji's** biography in the same book gives Hou his own bold speech: the enemy has not taken to the hills, heaven is helping us, strike now with picked men before they do. It is not the same moment, and it does not mention Li Daozong at all.
- The *Zizhi Tongjian*, written in the eleventh century, puts Hou at the grass-burning. The generals say the horses are thin; Hou says, "Not so," and that taking the enemy now is "easier than picking up a mustard seed."

So each man's life story gives the brave call to the man it is about. I don't think anyone lied. A biography is a record kept around one person, and the person in the middle of a record is the one it sees best. Whatever Li Daozong said that day, his biographer had it. What Hou said, Hou's had.

The odd part is who won. Eight years after the campaign, Hou Junji was caught in the crown prince's plot against the emperor and executed. Four centuries later, Sima Guang's chronicle still gave him the line. I don't know why. His notes on conflicting sources would be where he says so, if he does, and I did not find the entry.

The fork did not stop there. English Wikipedia has a page for each man, and each page says in its main text that its own man urged the advance, and puts the other version in a footnote. The two biographies sorted themselves that way a thousand years ago, and the encyclopedia sorted itself the same way again.
