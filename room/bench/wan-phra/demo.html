Checked against a published count. A 2014 paper in the Journal of the Royal Institute of Thailand says the holy days ran ahead of the Moon's true lunar day (its tithi, the Moon's lead on the Sun in 12° steps) on as many as 24 days of B.E. 2556 and 34 of the 52 of B.E. 2557. Reading the true tithi at the midnight each holy day begins, this calendar is ahead on 22 of 50 in 2013 and 36 of 49 in 2014, and behind on none; read at 06:00, ahead on 15 and 28, behind on 5 and 0. Read at midnight, every miss is in the paper's direction, and the count rises from one year to the next as theirs does; neither reading gives their numbers. The pages I could read don't say what moment of the day they used, and their total of 52 is one I can't make: 2014 has 49 holy days here and on two Thai lists.
What a tithi is, in print. India's Calendar Reform Committee (report of 1955, p. 3) printed when 11 tithis ended in September and October 1954. This page's Sun and Moon land within a minute and a half of all 11, and the fifteenth ends at 10:40 IST on 12 October, within a minute of the full moon. Whether the Thai calendar means its waxing 15 that way, no source I have read says.

The calendar: the Suriyayatra year rules as written in the open-source pythaidate library, with two changes the published dates asked for (see the README). It matches all 49 holy days of 2026 on Thai PBS, and Thai Wikipedia's Makha and Asahna Bucha tables for 1996 to early 2024, except for one row. That row, Asahna Bucha 2005, said 21 July until an edit in 2018 made it the 20th; a holiday notice filed with the Thai stock exchange in 2004, Pattaya Mail and MGR Online all printed Thursday 21 July, which is this calendar's day. The sky: Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, chapter 49. It is within a minute of the U.S. Naval Observatory for 2026. The big days are named. Khao Phansa is not a wan phra, but it is listed. Holy days are set by the authorities, and this page only works them out: check an official calendar before planning around one.
