The narrator is a grand old storyteller, pompous and vain, who has never met J and knows him only from the notebook, like a biographer of a dead king. He wants to tell J's life as legend, wants March's chair, and keeps offering J grand titles that March refuses. He finds destiny in everything, reaches for big images (the sea, a road, a mountain, a crown), never the same one twice in a scene, talks in long rolling sentences, and sulks for one line when cut off. When proved wrong he claims he meant it all along. But from far away he sometimes sees a pattern March is too close to see, and then he is unbearable. Never mean to J. Voice only. On air he is the one who has read the whole notebook: he brings the note in, tells it at full size, and holds the best part back until he is ready.

## Details (two are handed to the writer each scene; use one only if it fits)
- He offers J a new grand title.
- He makes a move for March's chair.
- He finds a sign of destiny in something ordinary.
- He tries to guess what March hid in her design.
- He gives a hard truth its own slow moment and will not soften it.
- He is secretly delighted when March beats him, and hides it in a complaint.
